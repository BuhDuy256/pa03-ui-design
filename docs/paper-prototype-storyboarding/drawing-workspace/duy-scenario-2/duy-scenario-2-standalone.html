2A — Haptic Pace Feedback

How it differs: feedback through vibration patterns — a channel that needs no attention at all. 2B uses music rhythm; 2C keeps the cue and changes only its timing.

2A is now a phone, not a watch. The pulses come from a phone on an armband or in a pocket. That is a weaker, less localised sensation than a watch against the wrist, so it makes the interpretation task harder — which is worth naming in WEAKNESSES / OPEN QUESTIONS rather than leaving for a grader to notice.

Prototype
2A — Haptic Pace Feedback
Problem
Looking at the screen to check pace breaks the runner's flow.
Motivation
5/7 PA2 participants rely on body feel over numbers; 4/7 reported visual checks as distracting.
How it works
1. Three distinct vibration patterns map to too fast / too slow / in range · 2. The phone pulses when pace leaves the target band · 3. The runner corrects without looking
Differs
Uses a channel that needs no attention at all. 2B uses music rhythm; 2C changes only when a cue fires.
Strengths
Fully eyes-free · works with music playing · no audio interruption
Weaknesses
Patterns must be learned · a phone on an armband or in a pocket gives a weaker, less localised pulse than a wrist device · open question: can users tell the patterns apart unaided?

Explanation card

Prototype
2B — Beat-to-Pace Matching
Problem
Checking pace visually breaks flow, and runners already use rhythm to regulate effort.
Motivation
Music is already playing. Turning its beat into the reference adds a feedback channel at zero extra cost in attention.
How it works
1. Target cadence sets a fixed beat · 2. Beat stays constant while pace drifts · 3. The runner hears the mismatch and re-syncs their footfall
Differs
Feedback is carried by music rhythm rather than a discrete cue. Nothing ever fires — the signal is continuous.
Strengths
No alert at all · uses a skill runners already have · continuous rather than episodic
Weaknesses
Only works with beat-driven music · mismatch may be too subtle to notice · cadence is not pace

2C — Just-in-Time Pace Alert

How it differs: same feedback, different timing rule — fires only after deviation persists. This is the one about when; Luân's 1C is the one about what a cue is made of. Both cards must say so.

Haptic-primary. The alert is the vibration. The screen is only what the runner would see if they looked, and they are never asked to. A visual alert would fail Scenario 2's own success condition.

Keep the stopwatch on your side of the table. A participant watching a counter climb is predicting the alert instead of judging whether it arrived at a reasonable moment.

Sheets 1–3 look nearly identical on purpose. The alert deliberately not firing is the whole prototype.

Explanation card

Prototype
2C — Just-in-Time Pace Alert
Problem
Alerts that fire on every small deviation interrupt more than they help.
Motivation
Pace naturally fluctuates. Treating every fluctuation as an event turns useful feedback into noise.
How it works
1. Deviation begins, system stays silent · 2. Silence holds while the deviation persists · 3. One haptic pulse fires only once it has lasted long enough to matter
Differs
Changes when a cue fires, not what it is made of. 1C changes the cue's modality — a tone instead of a voice. The two are not variants of each other.
Strengths
Far fewer interruptions · each alert carries real weight · tolerates natural pace variation
Weaknesses
Delay may feel like the system is broken · a correct threshold is unknown · late feedback may arrive after the moment has passed
