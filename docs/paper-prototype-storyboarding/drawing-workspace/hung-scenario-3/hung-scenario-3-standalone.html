3A — Run Lock Interface

How it differs: control stays on the phone but is locked behind a hold, so accidental input is impossible. 3B moves control to another device; 3C removes the target instead of locking it.

The lock is the idea — do not draw this as "bigger buttons". The original had nothing locked, which reduced 3A to a sizing tweak and would not survive a grader asking what makes it different from 3B and 3C. A tap does nothing. A hold is the only way in.

It also asked for a tap and then a hold — two deliberate steps for an action whose whole point is speed while running. Holding is the confirmation.

Facilitator — never in the participant stack

Say nothing. Let the silence be the feedback. Record: did they work out that a hold was needed, and how long it took.

Why it exists: an ignored tap is not an error in 3A — it is the feature working. But it is also the moment a participant is most likely to think the prototype is broken. How quickly someone moves from tapping to holding, unprompted, is the clearest measure of whether a locked interface is learnable.

Prototype
3A — Run Lock Interface
Problem
Small touchscreens invite mis-taps while running — sweat, motion, and a moving arm.
Motivation
5/7 PA2 participants reported in-run interaction friction. The cheapest fix is not a better button but no accidental button at all.
How it works
1. The run screen is locked — taps are ignored · 2. A deliberate hold is the only input, with a filling ring as feedback · 3. Only essential actions appear, at full width
Differs

Strengths
Accidental input is structurally impossible · large targets · hold gives clear feedback
Weaknesses
Slower than a tap · the hold must be discovered · still requires looking at the phone

3B — Earbud Controls

How it differs: control moves to a different device — the earbuds — so the phone never leaves the pocket.

"Pause" must say what it pauses. Pausing the music and pausing the run have completely different consequences — one is reversible in a second, the other loses tracking data. In 3B and 3C a double tap pauses music only; the run keeps recording, and the sheets say so. A participant guessing wrong produces a confused reaction that gets logged as a usability finding when it is really a wording bug.

3C — Voice-Free Quick Actions

How it differs: control stays on the phone but the target is removed — the whole screen is the button.

The real idea is "no target", not "no voice". 3A and 3B are equally voice-free, so the name distinguishes nothing. What 3C removes is aiming: nothing to hit, nothing to miss, which is the direct answer to the mis-tap complaint behind P3.

The name is kept because it is locked into PA3-Analysis.pdf, CLAUDE.md, and the video titles — renaming ripples further than it is worth. The explanation card states the real distinction instead.

Facilitator card — separate pile

Facilitator — never in the participant stack

Left: long-press read as a double tap, or the reverse. Right: deal unprompted, mid-run, with no gesture made at all.

Why it exists: 3C has a hole it should be tested against rather than hidden from. If any double tap anywhere pauses music, a phone jostling in a pocket or an arm brushing a sleeve fires it — the accidental-activation problem 3A solves with a lock and 3B avoids by leaving the phone alone entirely.

Deal the right-hand card once, unannounced, and record the reaction. It belongs in WEAKNESSES either way — discovering it in the session is far better than a grader discovering it in the report.

Explanation card

Prototype
3C — Voice-Free Quick Actions
Problem
Touch menus require aiming at a small target while the body is moving.
Motivation
If there is no target, there is no mis-tap. Removing aiming attacks the cause rather than enlarging the symptom.
How it works
1. The whole screen is the button · 2. Double tap anywhere pauses music, long press gives a pace cue · 3. No menu is ever opened
Differs
Keeps the phone but removes targeting. 3A keeps the target and locks it; 3B removes the phone from the interaction entirely. All three are voice-free — the name is historical.
Strengths
Nothing to aim at · fastest of the three · works with the phone in hand or through a sleeve
Weaknesses
No protection against accidental activation — a jostling pocket fires it · only two gestures fit · gestures can be confused with one another
